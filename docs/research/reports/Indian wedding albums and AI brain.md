# Teach the phone to read wedding stories

Every Indian wedding album, whatever the community, is built the same way: one decisive frame per ritual, the couple, every family group, and the emotional peaks (the farewell tears above all), cut down event by event from thousands of raw photos to roughly **80-300 printed photos on 30-40 sheets**. What changes from community to community is where the decisive frame sits and what it looks like. In many of them the most important photo is one a simple quality rule would throw away: the instant a screen comes down (Bengali betel leaves, the Marathi antarpat, the Parsi ara antar cloth, the Muslim arsi mushaf mirror), a nikah where bride and groom sit in **different rooms**, a gurdwara lavaan shot from behind with no flash, a Parsi lagan at 6:40 pm taken from a distance, or a grandparent who appears in only one function because they went home early. For the AI side, the research points one way. Generative vision-language models (VLMs) that can describe a photo are licence-clean in places (SmolVLM, Moondream, Florence-2, Gemma 4 E2B) but take an estimated **5-20 s per photo** on a Galaxy A15, which means hours for 2,500 photos. They can at most judge a short list. What fits the phone is one frozen **SigLIP 2 image encoder (Apache 2.0)**, which turns each photo into a number fingerprint (an embedding) at an estimated 0.2-0.35 s per photo. Small heads (tiny add-on scorers) trained on Claude's "which of these two is better" judgements sit on top of those fingerprints, and they feed a selection step that weighs quality against variety under per-event and per-person quotas. Apple's MobileCLIP, which earlier notes treated as a candidate, is **research-only** and cannot ship. Both the album numbers and the phone timings are weaker than they look: every source was read only through search-result summaries, and no vision model has yet been timed on a Helio G99 chip.

## One skeleton, a dozen different decisive frames

Across communities the album follows one arc: pre-wedding functions at the two homes, the arrival of the groom's party, a fixed-order ceremony with one or two peak actions, the farewell, then a reception or feast that is often a separate event days later. Indian guides list the same core shots everywhere: getting ready, outfit and jewellery close-ups, the garland exchange "amidst cheers and laughter", family portraits "start with the largest groups", reception reactions and the food ([WeddingBazaar](https://www.weddingbazaar.com/blog/wedding-moments-photo-checklist); [VenueLook](https://www.venuelook.com/blog/15-must-have-shots-in-your-wedding-album)). Three patterns run through the table, and each matters more to the app than any ritual name. The first is **the screen-then-reveal**. A Bengali studio calls shubho drishti "the single most important photograph" of the wedding and arrives 30 minutes early to secure a sightline to both faces ([Suvo Weddings](https://suvoweddings.netlify.app/blog-post-3.html)). The Marathi antarpat is removed so "the couple sees each other for the first time on their wedding day" ([VenueLook](https://www.venuelook.com/blog/marathi-wedding-traditions-all-you-need-to-know-about-their-beautiful-rituals)). The Parsi ara antar hides both faces behind a thick sheet until rice flies over it ([Today's Traveller](https://www.todaystraveller.net/a-journey-through-parsi-wedding-rituals/)). In the Muslim arsi mushaf the couple's first look comes through a mirror ([Fine Art Production](https://www.fineartproduction.com/muslim-wedding-photography)). In each case the build-up frames show hidden faces, and the prize is the first frame after the screen drops.

The second pattern is **speed against length**. South Indian peaks are seconds long. The Kerala thali is tied at a minute "calculated to the minute", inside a ceremony of 60-90 minutes ([itsmy.wedding](https://itsmy.wedding/blog/kerala-wedding-ceremony-sequence)). Telugu talambralu and the garland exchange "rarely pause for a second take" ([HiQ Weddings](https://www.hiqweddings.net/blog/telugu-wedding-photography-and-rituals)). Bengali mala badal is shot in burst mode ([Suvo Weddings](https://suvoweddings.netlify.app/blog-post-3.html)). Against these stand long, repetitive rites: the pheras, the four lavaan ([Jubily](https://www.jubily.co.uk/guides/anand-karaj-order-of-service)), the Meitei seven circles, and an hour of Parsi ashirvad blessing ([Today's Traveller](https://www.todaystraveller.net/a-journey-through-parsi-wedding-rituals/)). The app's "dense burst = big moment" rule has to treat a short, very dense burst as a peak and a long, steady run as one moment worth one or two frames.

The third pattern is **who is allowed where**. Where the photographer may stand shapes the raw folder before the app ever sees it. In "many communities" the bride sits with the women while a representative carries her consent to the nikah, and Kerala families often require a female photographer in purdah areas ([Camrin Films](https://camrinfilms.com/blog/muslim-wedding-photography-kerala)). Gurdwaras commonly ban flash during ardas and lavaan, and the photographer must never stand in front of the Guru Granth Sahib or make it the subject ([Royal Bindi](https://royalbindi.co.uk/things-to-take-care-of-while-taking-photographs-in-the-gurudwara/)). Non-Parsis are generally kept out of agiaries, so lagan photos are long-lens and distant ([Girish Lone](https://www.girishlone.com/behram--dhun.html)). Temple weddings such as Guruvayur's run from 5 am to 1 pm under tight space rules, so the clean couple shots come from portraits after the ceremony ([itsmy.wedding](https://itsmy.wedding/blog/guruvayur-temple-wedding-guide)).

## Emotion, elders and every family group decide what survives

What Indian photographers call album-worthy is emotional before it is technical. "There is no second take of an aunt crying during the vidaai" ([AccioPix](https://acciopix.com/blog/candid-vs-traditional-wedding-photography)). Guides list elders' blessings, parents' reactions, grandparents watching the ceremony and the couple's stolen glances as the classic candids ([WeddingSutra](https://www.weddingsutra.com/blog/30-emotional-wedding-moments-that-celebrate-family-bonds/); [WeddingWire India](https://www.weddingwire.in/wedding-tips/indian-family-photos--c9487)). The same holds outside Hindu weddings: rukhsati is "a mix of happiness and sadness" ([Visual Artistry Co](https://www.visualartistry.co/blog-1/69ggwzljs7gffkl-nyj5k-eglnj-yh5rh-j97w4-pxeaz-rl8xh-7zx8k-zljrs-2hddk-3blnt-d9p5r-89nxr-d7nnd-chbgt-xhm8f-tjd5d-3wfpk-fn4cc-49c64-s8p2t-z93gf)), and the Mangalorean opsun is the Catholic equivalent of vidaai ([Saral Marriage](https://blog.saralmarriage.com/sacred-rituals-mangalorean-catholic-wedding/)). Telugu photographers name "mothers with tears in their eyes" and grandmothers blessing the bride as the frames that matter ([Digiart Photography](https://digiartphotography.com/telugu-wedding-photography-hyderabad-2/)). So tears should earn a bonus, not just escape a penalty. The app's own notes already flag this as a gap ([HOW_IT_PICKS.md](../../HOW_IT_PICKS.md)).

Family groups are the other non-negotiable, and they are the shots that go missing most. Indian weddings are "notorious for delays, costume changes and back-to-back rituals", and grandparents end up "cold, tired and waiting for yet another group shot" ([Khush Wedding](https://khushwedding.com/inspiration/2026/02/05/10-big-wedding-photography-mistakes-brides-regret-and-how-to-avoid-them/)). The sharpest regret on record is having no photo with the grandparents at all, from a bride whose grandfather died three weeks after the wedding ([WeddingWire forum](https://www.weddingwire.com/wedding-forums/any-picture-regrets/6b8272dc2d26d0d2.html)). Guides say grandparents "should be specifically sought, not incidentally captured" ([Weddings Hub](https://weddingshub.co.uk/articles/wedding-photos-couples-regret-not-taking/)), and that three-generation portraits and grandparents from each side are standard ([WedMeGood](https://www.wedmegood.com/blog/building-a-photo-list-must-have-photos-you-dont-want-to-miss/)). A UK parent's complaint shows the other common failure: a whole side missing ("no photos of her daughter and her side of the family") despite a shot list ([Mumsnet](https://www.mumsnet.com/talk/am_i_being_unreasonable/4323721-To-be-feeling-guilty-about-key-missing-family-wedding-photos)). Archive albums show that reception stage sheets, with the couple standing beside guest after guest, are a long-standing Indian format ([British Library EAP946](https://eap.bl.uk/index.php/item/EAP946-27-1-1-363)). Every family that queued for the stage expects its one photo.

Named relatives recur by role, and the roles are not the same everywhere. The maternal uncle is central to the Gujarati mameru ([Kaushik Bathia](https://www.kaushikbathiaphotography.co.uk/blog-gujarati-wedding-traditions-photography)), gives the Punjabi chooda ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punjabi_wedding_traditions)), and in Khasi weddings blesses and formalises the union in place of a father figure ([Ka Jingshai](https://kajingshai.rkmshillong.org/postdetails.php?postId=286)). The bride's sisters run joota chupai and the Bengali bashor ghor door ransom ([Birdlens](https://www.birdlenscreation.com/blog/bengali-wedding-rituals)). Milni pairs uncle with uncle in descending order of age ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punjabi_wedding_traditions)). The Assamese juron brings the **groom's mother** to the bride's home ([Pernia's](https://www.perniaspopupshop.com/wedding-encyclopedia/assam)), which breaks any rule that guesses a person's side from "who shows up at the bride's pre-wedding events".

### Albums hold 80-300 photos, chosen event by event

The printed product is a 12x36-inch spread album sold by the sheet (one sheet is two 12x18 pages). Multi-event weddings typically take **30-40 sheets** so "each event breathe[s]" ([Photojaanic](https://www.photojaanic.com/blog/wedding-album-price-in-india-with-different-types-of-wedding-album-sheets)). Density is where the sources split. Dense traditional layouts average about 6 photos a sheet, so 40 sheets is roughly 240 photos ([AccioPix](https://acciopix.com/blog/wedding-album-size); [Album Delhi](https://albumdelhi.com/product/wedding-album-with-brown-printed-leather-bag-12x36/)). Modern "magazine" layouts use 2-3 per spread, and one guide argues that 80-100 strong photos on 30 sheets "looks better than a crowded one" ([Memoriffy](https://memoriffy.com/guide/wedding-photo-book-india)). Couples are commonly asked to shortlist 150-300 from 2,000-3,000 delivered photos ([pikperfect](https://www.pikperfect.com/blog/designing-a-beautiful-indian-wedding-album/)), and some guides go to 300-500 ([Prolab](https://www.prolab.in/blog/how-to-create-the-perfect-wedding-photo-album-complete-guide/)). Separate, smaller parents' albums are common ([The Photo Forum](https://www.thephotoforum.com/threads/wedding-album.160927)). Many parents still prefer traditional posed photos, which is why vendors sell "two styles, one team" ([FotoOwl](https://fotoowl.ai/blogs/the-rise-of-candid-wedding-photography-in-India-whos-leading-the-way)). The often-quoted **70% candid / 30% posed** split is a vendor figure, not a survey ([Sid Photo](https://sidphoto.in/candid-vs-traditional-wedding-photography/)).

Two things follow for the app. First, its default of about 15% (375 photos from a 2,500-photo folder) is a shortlist, not a printed album. That is the right job, since the Send screen hands the photographer a folder to design from, but couples and photographers think in sheets. A choice such as "classic, about 6 a sheet" against "modern, 2-3 a spread", tied to a sheet count, would speak their language. Second, the human process (reject blinks, blur and misfires; keep the best of each burst; then check the order) matches the app's pipeline ([Imagen](https://imagen-ai.com/valuable-tips/what-is-culling-in-photography/); [Narrative](https://narrative.so/blog/photo-culling-complete-faq)). Couples then shortlist event by event, in time order ([Folio Albums](https://www.folioalbums.com/how-to-choose-photos-for-the-perfect-wedding-album/)). The app's "what we left out" sheet should keep that same event-by-event grouping.

## What the selection must handle that simple rules miss

Rules such as "faces visible, eyes open, looking at the camera, couple together, everyone seen more than once" are right for most of the folder and wrong exactly at the moments families care about most. The table lists each case, with the evidence behind it and what the selection has to do instead.

|---|---|---|---|
| Screen-then-reveal | Bengali shubho drishti ([Jeet Biswas](https://jeetbiswasphotography.com/?p=19805)), Marathi antarpat ([WeddingWire India](https://www.weddingwire.in/wedding-tips/maharashtrian-wedding--c754)), Parsi ara antar ([Today's Traveller](https://www.todaystraveller.net/a-journey-through-parsi-wedding-rituals/)), arsi mushaf mirror and veil lift ([One Horizon](https://onehorizonproductions.com/muslim-wedding-photography/)) | "face hidden" drops the build-up; the peak looks like any couple frame | Inside a dense burst, find the change from hidden to visible faces, keep the first clear frame with both faces (bonus if they look at each other) and allow one build-up frame showing the screen |

A person forgives a flaw when the moment matters. A rule cannot, unless it knows the moment matters.

## Research favours pairwise teaching and diversity-aware picking over clever set models

The main academic line is "event-specific image importance": a photo's worth depends on the kind of event and on the other photos in the album. CUFED (1,883 Flickr albums, 23 event types) was built to learn this with a ranking loss ([CVPR 2016](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_cvpr_2016/html/Wang_Event-Specific_Image_Importance_CVPR_2016_paper.html)). The follow-up learns event type and importance together ([BMVC 2017](https://bmva-archive.org.uk/bmvc/2017/papers/paper094/index.html)). A user study confirmed that people's picks depend on the album around each photo ([Kuzovkin et al., ICMR 2018](https://inria.hal.science/hal-01934286v1/file/AlbumPhotoSelection_ICMR.pdf)). Two practical lessons follow: score photos relative to their own event, not on one global scale, and train from "which is better" pairs, not from star ratings.

The industrial systems that ship use exactly that recipe. Google Clips found raters "more consistent" choosing the better of two clips than giving scores. It collected more than **50 million pairwise comparisons** and distilled a large server model into a compact MobileNet ([Google Research](https://research.google/blog/automatic-photography-with-google-clips/)). Microsoft's burst picker trained on **838,038 labelled pairs** taken from within the same burst. The result is a **0.47 MB** model running in about 13 ms per frame on an iPhone 7, which matches the user's favourite frame **64.1%** of the time (top-3: **86.2%**) ([arXiv 1803.07212](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.07212)). Even a well-trained small model disagrees with the person's single favourite frame about a third of the time, so one-tap swapping in the viewer is part of the design, not a stopgap.

Models that look at the whole set barely beat simple methods. The best album summariser in an EMNLP 2017 study reached **45.5% precision** against human summaries, against **43.75%** for a determinantal point process (DPP) baseline, a classic "good but not alike" picker ([Yu et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1708.02977)). Another study found human summaries preferred only **59.5%** of the time over the strongest baseline ([arXiv 1604.04279](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1604.04279)). DPP and submodular objectives make near-duplicates unlikely to be picked together, and greedy approximations are the standard way to compute them ([Kulesza & Taskar](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1202.3738)). The conclusion is that a greedy "quality times importance, minus similarity to what is already picked" selection with quotas is close to the ceiling, and that the effort belongs in better per-photo scores and better similarity. This puts the app's measured **31-37% "same photo"** agreement with Claude's blind picks in context: it is within reach of what published systems achieve against humans, and "same moment" scores need care because picking every n-th photo matches them ([HOW_IT_PICKS.md](../../HOW_IT_PICKS.md)).

Teaching a small model from a big one now has direct evidence. PSDistill (May 2026) had a VLM make pairwise aesthetic judgements and turned them into calibrated scores with an Elo pool, a chess-style rating. It reached a rank correlation (SRCC) of 0.709, against 0.628 for majority vote and 0.557 for plain pairwise distillation ([arXiv 2605.19776](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.19776v1)). That was on paintings, its student was still a VLM, and a third-party summary questions possible data leakage ([Pith](https://pith.science/paper/2605.19776)). A separate paper distilled VLM pairwise preferences into a lightweight embedding student for picking the best image from a catalogue ([arXiv 2510.12014](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.12014v1)). Frozen CLIP-type embeddings with only a linear head already rank aesthetics well: SRCC 0.683 on AVA, against 0.574 for an ImageNet model ([Frontiers in AI](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/artificial-intelligence/articles/10.3389/frai.2022.976235/full)). The LAION aesthetic predictor is the same design ([LAION](https://laion.ai/blog/laion-aesthetics/)). Generic aesthetic scores are not album importance, though, and a 2026 audit finds LAION's predictor encodes a narrow "algorithmic gaze" ([arXiv 2601.09896](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.09896v4)). Nobody has published a VLM-labelled wedding-album study or a curve of how many labelled albums are enough.

The arithmetic rules out talking models for the main pass. On flagship CPUs, Gemma 3n E2B reads images at 110-163 tokens per second ([HF](https://huggingface.co/google/gemma-3n-E2B-it-litert-preview)), so a ~256-token image costs 1.6-2.3 s before the model writes a word. The G99 is several times slower, which puts a realistic 5-20 s per photo out of reach for 2,500 photos. A 2B model also needs 1.5 GB or more on top of the app's ~600 MB on 4 GB phones. SigLIP 2 B/32 runs through ONNX Runtime, which the app already ships. It can reuse the 1280-px decode the app already makes, and it needs only the vision tower on the phone: the text prompts are encoded once on the build machine and shipped as a small table of vectors. int8 compression keeps a CLIP B/32 almost identical to full precision (cosine 0.9957) at a quarter of the size ([ExecuTorch export](https://huggingface.co/mlboydaisuke/CLIP-ViT-B32-ExecuTorch)).

The project has already measured what a SigLIP 2 base encoder can and cannot do on wedding photos. Zero-shot, it recognised food (91%), mehendi (68-80%) and baraat (70-95%) well, but sindoor (17-37%) and pheras (17-33%) badly. A trained classifier dropped from 81% to **59%** once whole weddings were kept apart between learning and testing ([HOW_IT_PICKS.md](../../HOW_IT_PICKS.md)). Naming rituals is therefore the wrong job for the encoder, and the owner has ruled out ritual labels anyway. Its value lies elsewhere: similarity (near-duplicates and same-family-group checks across every photo), telling a jewellery close-up from an empty chair, spotting visual patterns such as "cloth held between two people" or "petals in the air" without ever showing a name, and above all serving as the input to a learned album-worthiness score.

## Recommended architecture: perception, a taught scorer, and a story planner

The recommended brain has three layers on the phone and one teacher off it. **Layer 1, perception**, keeps today's face stack (YuNet, Face Landmarker, SFace, EfficientDet) and adds one SigLIP 2 B/32@256 int8 embedding per photo, made from the existing 1280-px decode. That adds about 90 MB to the app and an estimated 0.2-0.35 s per photo, which keeps 2,500 photos within about 35-40 minutes on the A15 if the estimate holds. **Layer 2, per-photo understanding**, is a set of kilobyte-sized heads on that embedding plus the existing face and quality measurements. The most important head is an album-worthiness score trained with a pairwise ranking loss on pairs from within the same moment: those pairs hold the content constant and isolate "which frame", which is what Microsoft and Google relied on. The other heads are internal-only tags that never reach the screen: meaningful detail versus empty frame, posed group versus candid, showers in the air, a cloth or screen between two people, a couple at the altar seen from behind. Every score is normalised within its event, following the CUFED finding.

**Layer 3, the story planner**, stays rule-led and testable in pure Kotlin. It finds moments from shooting density (including very short bursts) and detects reveals inside a burst as hidden-to-visible face changes. It treats the two halves of a separated nikah as one moment and guarantees one frame per distinct family group, the elders, and side balance. Picking is greedy: learned score times importance, minus the embedding (and SFace) similarity to photos already picked, under quotas per event, moment and person. The literature says this is close to the human-agreement ceiling. The penalty caps from the earlier table go here, where they are visible and testable.

**The teacher** runs only on the build machine, never on the phone, so the app's no-internet rule is untouched. Claude judges same-moment pairs from harvested and real weddings, with reasons ("her eyes are half shut", "this is the moment the leaves came down"). The pairs become scores through calibrated Elo or Bradley-Terry (another pairwise rating model), and the heads are retrained in minutes. Teacher reasons that match a measured fault (a blink, talking, a hidden face) become extra training labels. The judge is the couple's real album, not the teacher: AlbumScore on wedding-1, with exact-photo precision reported next to moment recall, so results compare with the 45% literature ceiling. A small VLM (SmolVLM-256M or Gemma 4 E2B, both Apache) earns a place later, if at all, only on a 20-60 photo short list (cover choice, a sentence on why a photo was picked) and only on phones where Test Lab shows it fits in memory.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the problem. Album Done does not need a phone that can describe photos in words. It needs a phone that knows when to forgive. The frames Indian families value most are, again and again, the ones a quality checklist marks as flawed: hidden faces just before a reveal, a bride shown only among women, a blurry shower of petals, an elder seen once, a tearful aunt. A describing model cannot run on 2,500 photos on a 1-TOPS phone, and it would not fix this anyway. What fixes it is a cheap visual fingerprint for every photo and a scorer taught from many "this one, not that one" judgements within the same moment. The planner rules that say when a flaw is forgivable stay explicit and unit-tested.

The order of work follows from that. First, time SigLIP 2 B/32 int8 on the A15 in Test Lab, because every recommendation depends on that one number. Next, put the cheapest high-value rules into the planner (reveals inside bursts, the separated nikah, very short bursts, elders seen once, one frame per family group, capped occlusion), since they need no new model. Then build the pairwise teaching loop and judge it only against real couples' albums. A real album's page structure (photos per event, family-group share, detail share) remains the single most valuable missing data point. Its absence explains why most numbers in this report are still rules of thumb.
